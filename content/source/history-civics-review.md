# History and Civics (Review Questions)

## Meaning, importance and scope of History

The word history comes from the Greek word "historia", which means inquiry, or knowledge gained by asking questions. History is the study of the past of human beings: what people did, how they lived, and why things changed. The historian E. H. Carr called history an unending dialogue between the present and the past.

History is important for many reasons. It helps us understand the present, because today's world grew out of the past. It teaches us about our culture and heritage, and builds love for the nation. It shows us the mistakes and successes of earlier people, so that we can learn from them. It trains the mind to look at evidence, compare sources and think critically. It also builds tolerance, because we learn how different peoples and religions lived together.

The scope of History is very wide. It covers every period, from ancient times to the present day, and every place, from our village to the whole world. It studies every side of human life: politics, society, economy, religion, art, science and ideas.

## Different kinds of History

History can be divided by its subject. Political history studies kings, governments, wars and states. Social history studies family life, customs, caste, the position of women and daily life. Economic history studies agriculture, trade, industry and money. Cultural history studies art, literature, music and architecture. Religious history studies religions and religious movements. Constitutional history studies how laws and constitutions grew. Diplomatic history studies relations between countries. The history of science studies discoveries and inventions.

History can also be divided by area: local history, regional history, national history and world history. And it can be divided by time: ancient history, medieval history and modern history.

## Capitalism and Democracy

Capitalism is an economic system in which factories, land and businesses are owned by private people, not by the government. People produce goods to earn profit, and prices are decided by competition in the market. Adam Smith explained these ideas in his book The Wealth of Nations in 1776. Capitalism brought fast growth and new inventions, but it can also create a big gap between the rich and the poor.

Democracy comes from two Greek words: "demos", meaning people, and "kratos", meaning rule. So democracy means rule by the people. Abraham Lincoln described it as government of the people, by the people and for the people. In a direct democracy, the people decide laws themselves, as in ancient Athens. In a representative democracy, like India, the people elect representatives who make the laws. Democracy depends on free and fair elections, equality, the rule of law and freedom of speech.

## Citizenship

A citizen is a full member of a state, who enjoys its rights and performs duties towards it. Citizenship gives rights such as voting, contesting elections and the protection of the law, and it brings duties such as obeying laws, paying taxes and respecting the nation. Citizenship can be gained by birth, by descent, by registration or by naturalisation. Articles 5 to 11 of the Indian Constitution deal with citizenship, and the Citizenship Act of 1955 gives the details. India has single citizenship: every Indian is a citizen of India only, not of a separate state.

## The American Revolution

The American Revolution took place between 1775 and 1783. Thirteen British colonies in North America fought against Britain for their freedom. Britain put new taxes on the colonies, but the colonies had no members in the British Parliament. Their slogan was "No taxation without representation". In 1773, colonists threw British tea into the sea at Boston: this is called the Boston Tea Party. On 4 July 1776, the colonies adopted the Declaration of Independence, written mainly by Thomas Jefferson. George Washington led the colonial army. Britain accepted American independence by the Treaty of Paris in 1783. The revolution created the United States of America, and spread the ideas of liberty and democracy around the world.

## The French Revolution

The French Revolution began in 1789, when Louis the Sixteenth was king of France. French society was divided into three estates: the clergy, the nobles and the common people. The common people paid most of the taxes but had few rights. Thinkers such as Rousseau, Voltaire and Montesquieu spread new ideas of freedom and equality. On 14 July 1789, the people of Paris stormed the Bastille prison. The National Assembly passed the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The king was later executed and France became a republic. The revolution gave the world the ideals of Liberty, Equality and Fraternity.

## Nature and scope of Civics

The word civics comes from the Latin words "civis", meaning citizen, and "civitas", meaning city or state. Civics is the study of citizens: their rights, their duties, and how they are governed. It is a social science. It studies people living together in society, and it changes as society changes. It is practical, because it trains good citizens.

The scope of Civics covers the citizen and the family, schools and other social groups, local government such as the panchayat and the municipality, state and central government, and the nation. It also covers international bodies such as the United Nations. It studies rights and duties, the constitution, elections, political parties and public opinion.

## Salient features of the Indian Constitution

The Constitution of India was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950, which we celebrate as Republic Day. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar was the chairman of its Drafting Committee. It is the longest written constitution in the world.

Its main features are these. The Preamble declares India a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic. India has a parliamentary form of government. It is a federal system with a strong centre. Citizens have Fundamental Rights and Fundamental Duties, and the government is guided by the Directive Principles of State Policy. The judiciary is independent. Every citizen aged 18 or above can vote: this is universal adult franchise. There is single citizenship. The Constitution is partly rigid and partly flexible, so it can be amended when needed. India is secular: the state treats all religions equally.

## Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights are in Part Three of the Constitution, Articles 12 to 35. They protect the freedom and dignity of every citizen, and a citizen can go to court if they are violated. There are six Fundamental Rights. The Right to Equality: everyone is equal before the law, and untouchability is abolished. The Right to Freedom: freedom of speech, of assembly, of movement and of profession. The Right against Exploitation: forced labour and child labour in factories are banned. The Right to Freedom of Religion. Cultural and Educational Rights: minorities can protect their culture and run their own schools. The Right to Constitutional Remedies, Article 32: a citizen can go directly to the Supreme Court. Dr. Ambedkar called Article 32 the heart and soul of the Constitution. The Right to Property was removed from the list by the 44th Amendment in 1978.

## Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, on the advice of the Swaran Singh Committee. They are in Article 51A. There were ten at first; the eleventh was added in 2002. Some of them are: to respect the Constitution, the National Flag and the National Anthem; to protect the unity and integrity of India; to promote harmony and brotherhood; to respect women; to protect forests, rivers and wildlife; to develop a scientific temper; to protect public property and avoid violence; and for parents, to give their children between 6 and 14 years the chance of education.

## Directive Principles of State Policy

The Directive Principles of State Policy are in Part Four of the Constitution, Articles 36 to 51. The idea was taken from the Constitution of Ireland. They are guidelines for the government to make India a welfare state. They cannot be enforced in a court, but they are fundamental in governing the country.

They can be grouped into three kinds. Socialist principles: adequate means of livelihood for all, equal pay for equal work for men and women, and a living wage for workers. Gandhian principles: organising village panchayats, promoting cottage industries, and prohibiting harmful intoxicating drinks. Liberal principles: a uniform civil code for all citizens, care and education for young children, separating the judiciary from the executive, and promoting international peace.

## Organs of Government of India

The Government of India has three organs. The legislature makes laws. At the centre it is Parliament, made of the President, the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. The Lok Sabha is elected directly by the people for five years. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent house: one third of its members retire every two years.

The executive carries out the laws. The President is the head of the state, but real power is with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, who are responsible to the Lok Sabha.

The judiciary explains the laws, settles disputes and protects the rights of citizens. The Supreme Court is the highest court, and below it are the High Courts and the lower courts. The three organs keep a check on each other, so that no organ becomes too powerful.

## Teaching strategies for History and Civics

A good teacher uses many strategies to make History and Civics interesting. Storytelling brings past events to life for young children. The lecture method, with questions, gives a clear outline of a topic. Discussion and debate let students share and test their ideas. Dramatisation and role play, such as a mock parliament or a mock election, show how government really works. The project method lets students collect information and present it. The source method uses real documents, coins and pictures. Field trips to museums, monuments and the panchayat office give first-hand experience. Maps, timelines, charts, models and videos help students see places and time clearly.

In group discussion, every member should understand the goal of the discussion. They should prepare well, support their points with facts, and speak loudly and clearly enough for everyone to hear. They should not feel offended when the group does not accept their ideas. They should ask when they do not understand something, and support the decision of the group once it is made.
